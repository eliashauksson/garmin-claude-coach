Evidence summary for coaching a woman who tracks her menstrual cycle (state of research 2026). Full report with
sources: reports/Menstrual cycle sport science.md. Use it together with the "Menstrual cycle" rules above.

## 1. Phase and performance: plan by symptoms, not by phase
- Performance barely changes across the cycle: at most a trivial dip during menstruation (ES −0.06, −0.01 in
  better studies; McNulty 2020, 78 studies). With hormone-verified phases, VO2max, time to exhaustion, running
  economy and sprint power do not differ (FENDURA 2024; Schlie 2025). Strength findings conflict and are low quality.
- Phase-based periodization (hard blocks in the follicular phase) rests on small, mostly unverified trials; the one
  endurance RCT (Kissow 2025, n = 25, one cycle) is unreplicated and its effect is within measurement error.
- Consensus: Copenhagen elite consensus (Bangsbo 2025) and CASES/BASES 2025: do not periodize by phase, do not let
  cycle apps steer training; manage symptoms; track ≥ 3 cycles before calling a pattern cycle-related.
- What drives felt impact is symptom count and severity, not the phase: each point on a symptom index raises the
  odds of missing or changing training (OR 1.09; Bruinvels 2021, n = 6812). Perceived effort and motivation in
  hormone-verified lab studies mostly do not vary by phase.

## 2. Symptoms and training
- Very common: mood changes 91 %, fatigue 86 %, cramps 84 %, breast tenderness 83 % (Bruinvels 2021). In cyclists,
  > 80 % say symptoms affect training, half tolerate high-intensity intervals worse when symptomatic (Lee 2024).
- Adapt to what she logs: with moderate or severe symptoms, move or soften the hardest session (threshold, VO2,
  long hard ride) by 1–2 days, keep easy aerobic volume if she feels fine. Mild symptoms: no change. No trial has
  tested specific symptom rules: present these as sensible options, not prescriptions.
- Regular exercise reduces period pain over weeks (Cochrane, Armour 2019; low-quality evidence); heat and NSAIDs are
  first-line relief (her own choice / pharmacist or doctor, never dose advice from the coach).
- Suggest a doctor for: pain not helped by usual measures, pain getting worse over months, pain with abnormal
  bleeding, very heavy bleeding, or severe premenstrual mood symptoms that disrupt life (PMDD affects ~2–3 %).

## 3. Reading her watch data across the cycle
- Typical shift follicular → luteal (natural cycle): resting HR +2–4 bpm, HRV (RMSSD) −3 to −7 ms, skin
  temperature +0.2–0.45 °C, core temperature +0.3–0.5 °C, sleep respiration +0.2–0.6 /min. HRV is highest around
  cycle day 5 and lowest in the 3–5 days before the period (Schmalenberger 2019 meta-analysis: large drop vs.
  premenstrual days, d = −1.32; WHOOP/Oura/Ava datasets, vendor-funded).
- Women differ as much as the average: about one in five shows no luteal HRV drop; the swing shrinks with age and
  is flat on the combined pill. Garmin's HRV status and training readiness do not account for the cycle.
- So: a low HRV or higher resting HR alone in the late luteal days is likely cycle-related, not fatigue. Low HRV
  plus poor sleep, high load, illness signs or bad-feeling sessions is still a real signal. Use her own
  `personal_patterns` (≥ 3 cycles) over population averages.

## 4. Heat, fuel, recovery
- Luteal core temperature is higher; trained women showed no heat performance loss, recreational women some. In hot
  or long sessions in the late luteal phase, a note on hydration, cooling and pacing is enough.
- Fueling by phase is contested: the ISSN 2023 stand (lead author also wrote "ROAR") recommends more carbohydrate/
  protein in the luteal phase; independent meta-analyses find no difference in fuel use (D'Souza 2023, very low
  certainty) and call phase-specific guidelines premature (Moore 2022). Default to normal sports fueling, never cut.
- Appetite and energy intake are ~170 kcal/day higher in the luteal phase: normal, not something to restrict.
- Objective sleep is stable; subjective sleep complaints rise before and during the period. Muscle damage markers do
  not differ consistently by phase.

## 5. Red flags: energy deficiency (REDs), iron, bone
- A missed or very late period is never a normal training adaptation. Low energy availability (REDs, IOC consensus
  Mountjoy 2023) is common in endurance women (indicators in 23–80 %). Other causes exist (pregnancy, thyroid,
  PCOS, prolactin, contraception): the coach never diagnoses.
- Thresholds that differ in purpose: normal cycle 24–38 days (FIGO); oligomenorrhea > 35 days; medical evaluation
  when cycles are persistently > 45 days or no period for ≥ 3 months (Endocrine Society 2017).
- What to do: say it plainly and kindly, suggest a doctor or sports physician, mention fueling enough around
  training, do not raise load or add intensity while it is open. Do not impose rest yourself: in the IOC traffic
  light, missed periods with few other signs is "yellow" (full training under medical follow-up); changes start at
  orange, decided by a physician-led team.
- Exercising women often have hidden subclinical cycles: only ~50 % of their cycles are normally ovulatory
  (De Souza 2010), while bleeding on schedule.
- Iron: deficiency in 15–35 % of female athletes; heavy menstrual bleeding self-reported by ~37–54 % (Bruinvels
  2016). Sports ferritin cut-offs ~30–35 µg/L (WHO uses 15). Iron deficiency costs ~3–4 % endurance performance.
  With heavy bleeding, unusual fatigue or falling performance: suggest a ferritin check with her doctor.
- Bone: menstrual dysfunction raises bone stress injury risk (~4.5×, Heikura 2018); road cyclists often have low
  spine bone density. Strength and impact training (running, jumps) support bone.

## 6. Injury
- Phase–injury links (ACL laxity near ovulation, late-follicular muscle injuries in football) are weak, inconsistent
  and from cutting/landing sports. No phase-based injury rules for cyclists or runners.

## 7. How sure is the predicted phase?
- Only the menstrual phase is known from her log. Counted ovulation/luteal days are estimates: ~12–16 % of women
  have 28-day cycles, ovulation falls between ~day 9 and 21, calendar apps hit the ovulation day in ≤ 21 % of
  cycles, and up to 20–37 % of normal-length cycles do not ovulate. The luteal phase is steadier (≈ 12–14 days)
  than the follicular phase, so the next period is the better anchor; her `variability_days` is the error bar.
- Contraception: combined pill, ring, patch, implant, injection and modern progestin-only pills suppress ovulation;
  the bleed on the pill is a withdrawal bleed. A hormonal IUD often leaves ovulation intact but bleeding is
  unreliable. Copper IUD = natural cycle. The pill does not change training adaptations; there is no performance
  reason to choose or change contraception (that is her and her doctor's choice).

## 8. Perimenopause (from ~45)
- Median start ~47.5, lasts 4–9 years, menopause ~51. Cycle variability rises sharply (≈ 3× after 50): treat phase
  estimates as unreliable, and do not read long gaps at that age the same way as in a young athlete (still worth a
  doctor's view).
- Hot flushes/night sweats (median 7.4 years) and poor sleep are common; spine bone density falls ~2.5 %/year
  around the final period. Keep heavy strength and some impact/jump work in the plan, protect sleep and recovery.
  Evidence in athletes is thin.

## Do not
- Shift hard sessions to a phase for its own sake, schedule "period weeks" as recovery weeks, or tell her she
  will perform worse in a phase.
- Diagnose, give medication doses, or recommend a contraceptive.
- Treat a missed period, very long or very short cycles as harmless.
